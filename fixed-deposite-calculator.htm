What Is a Fixed Deposit and Compound Interest?

A fixed deposit (FD), also called a term deposit, is a savings product in which you deposit money for an agreed period under specified terms. The bank or financial institution pays interest according to the conditions of that deposit. The interest you receive depends on the offered rate, the deposit period, the interest calculation method and the payout option.

Compound interest is a process in which accumulated interest itself earns interest in later periods. If interest is added to the balance and stays invested, later interest calculations use the larger balance rather than only the original principal. Simple interest, by contrast, is calculated on the original principal alone.

Use this calculator to explore an estimated maturity amount from your starting deposit, annual nominal interest rate, period and compounding frequency. The result rests on the assumptions below and is not a guaranteed quote from any particular bank.

How to Use the FD Calculator

  1. Enter the principal: the amount you plan to deposit, for example 10000.
  2. Enter the annual interest rate: if the stated rate is 7.1% a year, enter 7.1, not 0.071. A rate of 0 is allowed.
  3. Enter the period in years: 5 means five years. Decimals such as 2.5 are accepted.
  4. Select the compounding frequency: yearly, half-yearly or quarterly.
  5. Review the result: it updates automatically whenever you change an input.

If a value is missing or outside its allowed range, the result area names the field to fix. Allowed ranges: principal greater than 0 up to 10,000,000,000; rate from 0% to 100%; period from 0.1 to 100 years. The tool is currency-neutral, so enter amounts in whatever currency your deposit is in.

The Math Behind the Calculator

This calculator uses the standard formula for a single lump-sum deposit earning a fixed nominal annual rate with periodic compounding:

A = P × (1 + r/n)nt
  • A: the estimated final amount, including principal and accumulated interest.
  • P: the initial principal deposited.
  • r: the annual nominal rate as a decimal. For example, 7.1% is 0.071.
  • n: compounding periods per year: 1 for yearly, 2 for half-yearly, 4 for quarterly.
  • t: the period in years.

The periodic rate is r ÷ n and the number of periods is n × t. If the period is not a whole number of compounding periods, the calculator keeps the fractional exponent, whereas a bank may apply its own rule for the incomplete period. Interest earned is A − P. The effective annual yield is (1 + r/n)n − 1, which shows what the nominal rate is worth after compounding for one year.

Worked Example: A 10,000 Deposit

Suppose you deposit 10,000 at a fixed nominal rate of 7.1% for five years with quarterly compounding. The periodic rate is 7.1% ÷ 4 = 1.775% and there are 20 quarterly periods.

A = 10,000 × (1 + 0.071 ÷ 4)20 ≈ 14,217.47

The interest earned is 14,217.47 − 10,000 = 4,217.47. The effective annual yield is (1.01775)4 − 1 ≈ 7.29%, slightly above the 7.1% nominal rate because interest is added four times a year. These figures demonstrate the calculator's model; they do not establish the maturity amount payable under any bank's FD product.

Important Assumptions and FD Limitations

  • The rate is fixed and nominal for the entire period. Rate changes, premature closure, renewal, penalties and fees are not modeled.
  • There is one initial lump-sum deposit and no additional deposits or withdrawals, and interest stays invested.
  • Banks may use different rules for incomplete periods, day counts and rounding, so their maturity quotation can differ slightly from this result.
  • Tax, tax deducted at source and inflation are not deducted. Inflation lowers the purchasing power of the maturity value.
  • The calculation runs entirely in your browser; the values you enter are not sent anywhere.

Cumulative and periodic-interest payout deposits

A cumulative deposit generally retains interest toward maturity, subject to the product's rules. A periodic-interest payout deposit pays interest at scheduled intervals, so that money no longer stays in the deposit to earn further interest. This calculator models the growth of a single balance; it does not model the timing or reinvestment of periodic payouts.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is compound interest different from simple interest?

Simple interest is calculated on the original principal, while compound interest is calculated on the principal and previously accumulated interest. The difference grows over longer periods, depending on the rate and compounding method.

How do I calculate fixed deposit maturity?

This calculator estimates maturity by multiplying the principal by one plus the periodic interest rate, raised to the total number of compounding periods. Actual bank calculations may use additional product-specific rules.

What does quarterly compounding mean?

Quarterly compounding means interest is compounded four times per year under the selected mathematical model. This calculator divides the nominal annual rate by four and uses four periods for each year.

Will this result exactly match my bank's FD maturity amount?

Not necessarily. Actual maturity can depend on the bank's interest calculation method, deposit terms, payout option, incomplete-period rules and rounding method. Check the bank's official maturity quotation.

Can I calculate an FD with a zero interest rate?

Yes. With a zero interest rate and no additional deposits or withdrawals, the estimated maturity amount equals the original principal.

What is the difference between cumulative and periodic-interest payout FDs?

A cumulative FD generally accumulates interest toward maturity, while a periodic-interest payout FD pays interest at scheduled intervals. Their cash flows and maturity proceeds differ. This calculator estimates a growing balance rather than periodic payouts.

Is tax included in the maturity estimate?

No. The estimate does not deduct tax, withholding, penalties, fees or other bank-specific adjustments. Your actual after-tax return can differ from the amount shown.

What is the Rule of 72?

The Rule of 72 is a rough mental estimate of doubling time: divide 72 by the annual interest rate as a percentage. At 6% the estimate is about 12 years. Actual growth depends on the rate, compounding method and applicable terms.
