What Is a Fixed Deposit and Compound Interest?

A fixed deposit (FD), also called a term deposit, is a savings product in which you deposit money for an agreed period under specified terms. The bank or financial institution pays interest according to the conditions of that deposit. The interest you receive depends on factors such as the offered rate, the deposit period, the interest calculation method, and the payout option.

Compound interest describes a mathematical process in which accumulated interest can itself earn interest in later periods. If interest is added to the balance and remains invested, subsequent interest calculations may use the increased balance rather than only the original principal. This can make compound growth different from simple interest, which is calculated on the original principal alone.

The Math Behind the Calculator

This calculator uses the standard formula for a single lump-sum deposit earning a fixed nominal annual rate with periodic compounding:

A = P × (1 + r/n)nt

In this formula:

  • A: The estimated final amount, including the original principal and accumulated interest.
  • P: The initial principal deposited.
  • r: The annual nominal interest rate expressed as a decimal. For example, 7.1% is represented by 0.071.
  • n: The number of compounding periods per year. It is 1 for yearly, 2 for half-yearly, and 4 for quarterly compounding.
  • t: The investment period expressed in years.

The periodic rate in this model is r divided by n, while the total number of periods is n multiplied by t. The calculation uses the selected frequency and retains fractional exponents if the entered duration does not correspond to a whole number of periods. Actual financial institutions may apply different rules for incomplete periods.

How Compounding Frequency Affects Growth

Compounding frequency determines how often interest is added to the balance in the mathematical model. For the same positive nominal annual rate, the formula generally produces a higher final amount when compounding occurs more frequently, assuming the same principal and time period. This comparison depends on the model's assumptions and should not be used to conclude that a bank product with a particular payout schedule is automatically better.

  • Yearly: The model compounds interest once per year.
  • Half-yearly: The model compounds interest twice per year.
  • Quarterly: The model compounds interest four times per year.

When comparing actual deposits, review the effective terms and quoted rates for each product rather than relying only on the frequency label. Banks can use product-specific calculation conventions, and the nominal rate alone may not tell the entire story.

Important Assumptions and FD Limitations

The result assumes a fixed annual nominal rate for the entire entered duration, the selected periodic compounding frequency, one initial lump-sum deposit, and no additional deposits or withdrawals. It also assumes the calculated interest remains invested according to the formula. The tool does not model changes to rates, premature closure, renewal, penalties, fees, or tax deductions.

Actual fixed-deposit maturity can differ because financial institutions apply the terms of their specific deposit products. These terms may determine the interest calculation method, the handling of incomplete periods, the payout schedule, and the rounding of interest. A bank's actual maturity quotation may therefore differ from the result displayed here even if the starting amount and advertised rate look the same.

Cumulative and periodic-interest payout deposits

A cumulative deposit generally retains interest toward maturity, subject to the product's rules. A periodic-interest payout deposit instead pays interest at scheduled intervals. Because money paid out may no longer remain in the deposit to earn further interest, the cash-flow pattern differs. This calculator models growth of a single balance; it does not calculate the timing or reinvestment of periodic payouts.

Rounding, taxes, and incomplete periods

This calculator rounds the displayed monetary result to two decimal places. It does not reproduce a bank's internal rounding at every calculation step. It also does not estimate tax liability or deductions, and it does not model every possible day-count convention or incomplete compounding period. Check the official terms and maturity quotation for the FD you intend to open.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. How is compound interest different from simple interest?

Simple interest is calculated on the original principal, while compound interest is calculated on the principal and previously accumulated interest. The difference can become more significant over longer periods, depending on the rate and compounding method.

2. How do I calculate fixed deposit maturity?

This calculator estimates maturity by multiplying the principal by one plus the periodic interest rate, raised to the total number of compounding periods. Actual bank calculations may use additional product-specific rules.

3. What does quarterly compounding mean?

Quarterly compounding means interest is compounded four times per year under the selected mathematical model. This calculator divides the nominal annual rate by four and uses four periods for each year.

4. Will this result exactly match my bank's FD maturity amount?

Not necessarily. Actual maturity can depend on the bank's applicable interest calculation method, deposit terms, payout option, duration, incomplete-period rules, and rounding method. Check the bank's official maturity quotation.

5. Can I calculate an FD with a zero interest rate?

Yes. With a zero interest rate and no additional deposits or withdrawals, the estimated maturity amount equals the original principal. The calculator supports a zero interest rate.

6. What is the difference between cumulative and periodic-interest payout FDs?

A cumulative FD generally accumulates interest toward maturity, while a periodic-interest payout FD pays interest at scheduled intervals. Their cash flows and maturity proceeds differ. This calculator estimates a growing balance rather than periodic payouts.

7. Is tax included in the maturity estimate?

No. The estimate does not deduct tax, withholding, penalties, fees, or other bank-specific adjustments. Your actual after-tax return can differ from the amount shown by the calculator.

8. What is the Rule of 72?

The Rule of 72 is a rough mental estimate of doubling time. Divide 72 by an annual interest rate expressed as a percentage. For example, at 6% the estimate is about 12 years. Actual growth depends on the rate, compounding method, and applicable terms.
