SIP Calculator – Monthly Investment Returns

Estimate the maturity value of a monthly SIP at a constant expected return.

What a SIP Calculator Does

A Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) invests a fixed amount in a mutual fund every month instead of putting in one large lump sum. Most investors know that starting early helps, but few can say by how much. This calculator takes your monthly amount, the annual return you expect and the number of years you will stay invested, and returns the estimated maturity value, the total amount you put in and the estimated returns.

It assumes one constant annual return for the whole period. Real mutual fund returns move up and down from year to year, so treat the output as a planning estimate, not a forecast or a promise.

How to Use the Calculator

  1. Enter the amount you will invest each month, or drag the slider. Typed values can exceed the slider's range.
  2. Enter the expected annual return as a percentage. A rate of 0 is allowed and simply adds up your contributions.
  3. Enter the investment period in whole years.
  4. Press Calculate. After the first calculation the result refreshes as you edit any value.
  5. Press Reset to return to the defaults (₹5,000 a month at 12% for 10 years) and clear the result.

If a value is missing or outside its allowed range, the result area names the field to fix. Allowed ranges: monthly investment greater than 0 up to ₹10,00,00,000; return from 0% to 50%; period from 1 to 60 whole years.

The SIP Formula and Its Symbols

FV = P × [((1 + i)n − 1) ÷ i] × (1 + i)
  • P – the amount invested each month.
  • i – monthly rate as a decimal: annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100.
  • n – number of monthly instalments: years × 12.
  • FV – estimated maturity value.

This is the future value of an annuity due: each instalment is assumed to be invested at the start of its month. Your first instalment compounds for all n months and your last for just one, which is why the early instalments do most of the work. Many online SIP calculators assume month-end investing instead, which gives a slightly lower figure; neither is wrong, they model different timing. When the rate is exactly zero the formula would divide by zero, so the calculator uses FV = P × n. Total invested is P × n and estimated returns are FV − total invested. Amounts are computed at full precision and rounded to whole rupees only for display.

Worked Example

Take ₹5,000 a month at 12% a year for 10 years. Then i = 12 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.01 and n = 120. The growth factor is (1.01)120 ≈ 3.300387, so ((1.01)120 − 1) ÷ 0.01 ≈ 230.0387. Multiplying by (1 + i) gives ≈ 232.3391, and 5,000 × 232.3391 ≈ ₹11,61,695.

You invested 5,000 × 120 = ₹6,00,000, so the estimated returns are about ₹5,61,695. That is roughly 1.94 times the money you put in. The figures are an illustration at a constant 12%, not a prediction.

Practical Uses

  • Goal planning: work backwards by trying monthly amounts until the maturity value reaches your target for education, a home down payment or retirement.
  • Testing return assumptions: run the same plan at a cautious rate and an optimistic rate to see the range of outcomes.
  • Deciding when to start: compare starting now with starting a few years later to see the cost of waiting.
  • Sizing a SIP increase: check how much a higher monthly amount shortens the time to your goal. This calculator keeps the amount constant, so run a larger figure to compare.

Assumptions and Limitations

  • The return is a constant annual rate. Actual market-linked returns vary year to year and can be negative.
  • Monthly compounding at one-twelfth of the annual rate, with each instalment invested at the start of its month.
  • The monthly amount is fixed. Annual step-ups, missed instalments and lump-sum top-ups are not modeled.
  • Exit loads, expense ratios, taxes and inflation are not deducted. In real terms, the purchasing power of the maturity value will be lower than the rupee figure.
  • The calculation runs entirely in your browser; the values you enter are not sent anywhere.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a SIP and how does a SIP calculator work?

A Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) invests a fixed amount at regular monthly intervals. A SIP calculator estimates the maturity value by compounding each monthly instalment at the expected rate of return for the remaining months of your investment period.

Are SIP returns guaranteed?

No. Mutual fund returns are market-linked and not guaranteed. The calculator shows an estimate based on the constant annual return you enter. Actual returns will vary.

Which formula does this SIP calculator use?

It uses the future value of an annuity due: FV = P × [((1 + i)^n − 1) ÷ i] × (1 + i), where P is the monthly investment, i is the monthly rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100) and n is the total number of months.

Why does my fund's SIP statement differ from this calculator?

A real SIP buys units at each month's NAV on the instalment date, so the result depends on actual market movements, not a smooth constant rate. Timing (start or end of month), fees and taxes also differ, so small or large differences are normal.

What return rate should I assume?

There is no single correct rate. Use a cautious figure for planning and test a lower and a higher rate to see the range. Past returns of a fund or index do not guarantee future returns.

Is a SIP better than a lump sum?

It depends on your cash flow and on market behavior over the period. A SIP spreads purchases over time and fits a monthly income; a lump sum puts all money to work at once. This calculator models only the SIP case.

Conclusion

A SIP is built from three things you control: how much you invest each month, how long you stay invested and the return you earn. Of these, time is usually the strongest lever, because the later instalments compound for only a short while while the earliest ones do most of the work. Use the calculator to test a few scenarios, keep your assumed return conservative, and remember that it assumes a constant rate and ignores fees, taxes and inflation. Check the fund's scheme documents before investing, because actual returns will vary with the market.
